Four Gods and a Carving Knife

The faces on our walls have older names than 'tiki bar.' A short, respectful tour of the gods and ancestors behind the carvings, and what the word tiki actually means.

Start with the word. In Māori tradition, Tiki is the first man, in some tellings shaped from red clay by the god Tāne. In the Marquesas, tiki are carved figures of deified ancestors, guardians rendered in wood and stone with wide eyes and heavy hands. Hawaiians used a different word for their carved images: kiʻi. What Americans came to call "tikis" in the twentieth century is really a whole family of Polynesian carving traditions, folded (not always carefully) into one borrowed name.

Behind the carvings stood real theology. Hawaiʻi honoured four great akua, four faces of the world's workings, and knowing them changes how you look at every carved face in this room.

The four great akua

Kū is effort and struggle: war, politics, fishing, medicine, the rising sun. His carvings snarl: wide mouth, defiant chin. Temples of Kū demanded the most serious offerings, and his season was the season of work and conflict.

Kāne is the creator: sunlight, fresh water, life itself. Where Kū takes, Kāne gives. Springs and rain clouds belonged to him, and no blood was offered at his shrines; he was honoured with water and prayer.

Lono is peace and plenty: harvest, rain, music, games. For four months a year, during the Makahiki festival, war was simply forbidden: taxes were paid in food, sport replaced fighting, and Lono's image travelled the islands. When Captain Cook sailed into Kealakekua Bay in the middle of Makahiki season in 1779, the timing shaped everything that followed, a collision of calendars that historians still argue about.

Kanaloa is the deep ocean: the long voyage, the squid, the tide. Companion and counterpart to Kāne, he watched over the sea roads that connected the largest nation of navigators the world has ever known.

A carving was never the god. It was a vessel the god might visit, a doorway kept open by respect.

What a bar owes the gods

Mid-century America borrowed these faces for restaurants and bowling alleys, and much was lost in the borrowing. The modern tiki revival tries to do better: to treat the carvings as craft with a lineage, not props. Our own totems were carved by hand in Bali, a different island tradition invited here deliberately, the way Lao silk and Jamaican rum share our menu. We keep the faces because they mean something: effort, generosity, peace, the deep water. A bar could organise itself around worse ideas.

So when the totems by the door catch your eye tonight, give them a nod. They have been watching over gatherings like this one, in one form or another, for a very long time.
